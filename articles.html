LANDSLIDE BASICS

1. What causes a landslide?

Landslides happen when rock, soil or debris moves down a slope under gravity. A slope can become unstable when the forces pulling material downhill become greater than the forces holding it in place. Triggers include intense or prolonged rainfall, earthquakes, river erosion, construction and changes to the land.

Rain can add weight to soil and increase water pressure between soil particles, reducing the strength that helps keep a slope stable. However, not every landslide has the same cause, and local geology, slope shape, drainage and land use all matter.

Why it matters: Understanding local conditions helps communities recognise risk and make safer decisions about where to build, travel and evacuate.

WEATHER & ENVIRONMENT

2. How heavy rainfall affects slope stability

During long or intense rain, water can soak into the ground and make soil heavier. Water pressure may build up in the spaces between soil grains, reducing friction and making movement more likely. Surface runoff can also erode the base of a slope or concentrate water in one place.

Keep drainage channels clear where safe, avoid directing roof or road runoff onto unstable slopes, and pay attention to official weather and hazard information. Do not attempt engineering work on a slope without qualified advice.

EARLY WARNING

3. Warning signs communities should recognise

Possible signs include new cracks in the ground or walls, bulging soil, leaning trees or poles, unusual water seepage, sudden changes in stream levels and rumbling or cracking sounds. These signs do not prove that a landslide will happen, but they should be taken seriously.

Move away if danger is immediate and report observations to local authorities. Some landslides happen with little or no visible warning, so never rely on warning signs alone.

COMMUNITY SAFETY

4. Why evacuation planning matters

Roads can become blocked by mud, rocks or damaged bridges during a landslide. Planning more than one route, identifying safer meeting points and keeping emergency contacts accessible can save valuable time.

Families should consider the needs of children, older adults, people with disabilities and pets. Practise the plan before the rainy season and follow evacuation instructions from local authorities.

RISK REDUCTION

5. How communities can reduce landslide risk

Risk reduction begins with understanding local terrain and avoiding unsafe development on unstable slopes or in channels where debris may travel. Protecting suitable vegetation, managing stormwater and maintaining drainage can help reduce some slope-related problems.

Large retaining structures, slope modifications and drainage designs require assessment by qualified geotechnical professionals. Simple actions by residents should never be treated as a substitute for professional engineering or official land-use guidance.

Landslide risk in Myanmar

Myanmar has mountainous and hilly regions where steep terrain, weathering, intense monsoon rainfall and changes to land cover can contribute to landslide hazards. Risk varies greatly from one location to another. Communities should use local knowledge alongside information from relevant authorities and qualified specialists.
